How to Answer the Strengths and Weaknesses Question in a Norwegian Job Interview

Published November 5, 2026

Sooner or later in a job interview in Norway, someone will ask about your strengths and weaknesses. If the interview is in Norwegian, it often sounds like "Hva er dine sterke og svake sider?" (What are your strong and weak sides?). Many international job seekers find this question uncomfortable. Some oversell, some freeze, and some give a rehearsed answer that sounds false.

The good news is that a calm, honest answer usually works best in Norway. This article shows you how to prepare one, with example answers you can adapt.

What Norwegian interviewers listen for

The question is rarely a trap. The interviewer wants to find out three things:

  • Self-awareness: do you know what you are good at and where you struggle?
  • Honesty: does your answer sound real, or memorised?
  • Fit: do your strengths match what the job actually needs?

Norwegian workplaces tend to value modesty, directness and teamwork. Big claims such as "I am the best salesperson you will ever meet" can sound arrogant. On the other hand, being too modest is also a problem, because the interviewer needs to hear what you bring. Aim for the middle: state your strengths plainly and back them up with a short example.

How to talk about your strengths

Choose two or three strengths, not ten. Pick ones that match the stillingsannonse (job ad). If the ad asks for someone who is selvstendig (independent), punktlig (punctual) or serviceinnstilt (service-minded), those words tell you what to focus on.

For each strength, use a simple pattern:

  1. Name the strength.
  2. Give one concrete example.
  3. Connect it to this job.

Before (vague):

I am hard-working, flexible and a team player.

After (concrete):

I am reliable. In my last job at a warehouse, I was often asked to cover extra shifts at short notice because my manager knew I would turn up on time. I understand this role has early starts, and that suits me well.

Here is another example, from Amira, who is applying for an assistant job at a care home:

One of my strengths is patience. While I was studying, I helped look after my grandmother for two years, and I learned to stay calm when things take longer than planned. I think that is important when working with elderly residents.

Notice that neither answer uses superlatives. The example does the convincing.

How to talk about a weakness honestly

This is the harder half. A good answer has three parts:

  1. A real weakness that is not central to the job.
  2. A short example of how it shows up.
  3. What you are doing about it.

Avoid the classic fake weakness, such as "I work too hard" or "I am a perfectionist". Norwegian interviewers have heard these many times, and they can make you seem less honest.

Example for an office job:

I can find it hard to say no when colleagues ask for help, and then my own tasks get delayed. I have started writing a short priority list every morning, and if someone asks me for help, I check it before I say yes.

Example for a café or shop job:

I used to get stressed when there was a long queue. In my last job, I asked a more experienced colleague how she handled busy periods, and now I focus on one customer at a time. It has made a big difference.

Example for someone new to the Norwegian job market:

I am still learning how things are done in Norwegian workplaces, for example how meetings work and how direct people are with feedback. I ask questions when I am unsure, and I find that colleagues appreciate it.

Do not choose a weakness that is the core of the job. If you apply for an accounting role, "I am not very good with numbers" will end the conversation.

When your weakness is your Norwegian

Many ads ask for Norwegian skills, and many international applicants worry about this. If your Norwegian is not yet strong, it is usually better to mention it openly than to hope nobody notices. The interviewer will find out anyway.

Be specific about your level. If you know your level on the CEFR scale (A1–C2), use it. If not, use plain words such as basic, conversational or fluent. Then say what you are doing to improve.

My Norwegian is at about B1 level. I can handle everyday conversations with customers, but I sometimes need people to repeat themselves if they speak a strong dialect. I attend an evening course twice a week and I speak Norwegian at home with my partner.
My Norwegian is basic at the moment, so I work in English for now. I am taking a course and I practise with colleagues during breaks. I would be happy to use Norwegian more as I improve.

Never claim a higher level than you have. It can damage trust quickly, and your references may be asked about it.

Answers that tend to backfire

Watch out for these common mistakes:

  • "I don't have any weaknesses." This sounds either dishonest or unaware.
  • Too much detail. Keep each answer to about a minute. This is not a confession.
  • Criticising a former employer. "My weakness is that my last boss never explained anything" shifts blame and sounds negative.
  • Strengths with no evidence. "I am a great leader" means little without an example.
  • A weakness with no plan. Ending with "...and that's just how I am" leaves a bad impression.

Use the job ad to prepare your answer

The best preparation starts with the job ad, not with a list of generic strengths. Print the ad or open it on your screen, and:

  1. Highlight the qualifications and personal qualities it asks for.
  2. Next to each one, write a short example from your work, studies or volunteering.
  3. Mark the requirements you do not fully meet. One of these, if it is not central, can become your honest weakness.

This also prepares you for follow-up questions. A Norwegian interviewer may ask, "Can you give an example?" or "How would your colleagues describe you?" If you have done this exercise, you will have answers ready.

It also helps to keep your answers consistent with what you wrote in your søknad (application). If your application says you are well organised, use that as one of your strengths in the interview. Summary

  • Pick two or three strengths that match the job ad, and support each with a short, concrete example.
  • Choose a real weakness that is not central to the job, give an example, and explain what you are doing about it.
  • If your Norwegian is still developing, describe your level honestly and say how you are improving.
  • Keep the tone modest but clear, and keep each answer short.
  • Prepare from the job ad so that your answers fit the role you are applying for.

Frequently asked questions

How many strengths should I mention in a Norwegian interview?

Two or three is usually enough. It is better to explain a few strengths well, with examples, than to list many without any evidence. If the interviewer wants more, they will ask.

Can I say that my Norwegian is a weakness?

Yes, if it is true, and it is often wise to be open about it. Describe your level honestly, for example with the CEFR scale or words like 'basic' or 'conversational', and explain what you are doing to improve. That shows self-awareness and effort.

Is it okay to give the same answer in every interview?

You can reuse the structure, but adapt the content to each job. A strength that matters in a warehouse may be different from one that matters in a kindergarten or an office. Look at the job ad each time and pick examples that fit.

What if I am asked about weaknesses in Norwegian and I don't understand the question?

It is fine to ask the interviewer to repeat or rephrase it, or to ask if you may answer in English. Listen for the words 'sterke sider' (strengths) and 'svake sider' (weaknesses). Asking politely is much better than guessing.

Should my weakness be something from work or from my personal life?

Choose something related to work, studies or how you work with others. Very personal weaknesses are usually not relevant and can make the conversation awkward. Keep it professional and connected to the kind of job you are applying for.
