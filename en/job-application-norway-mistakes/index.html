Job Application Mistakes to Avoid When Applying in Norway (and How to Fix Them)

Published October 17, 2026

If you are applying for jobs in Norway in English, small mistakes can make a good candidate look like a poor fit. Many of these mistakes come from writing the way you would in another country. A Norwegian job application (søknad) is usually short, direct and honest. This guide goes through the most common mistakes and shows how to fix each one, with examples you can adapt.

Mistake 1: Sending the same application to every job

It is tempting to write one good letter and send it to every job ad (stillingsannonse) you find on FINN.no, arbeidsplassen.no or LinkedIn. Employers usually notice quickly when a letter could have been sent to anyone.

Before:

I am a hard-working and motivated person looking for new challenges in a good company.

After:

I am applying for the warehouse assistant position at Y AS because your ad mentions early shifts and forklift work. I have two years of experience working in a warehouse in Poland, including forklift driving, and I am happy to work early mornings.

The fix is simple: pick two or three requirements from the ad and show, with concrete examples, that you meet them. Use the employer's name and the job title they use.

Mistake 2: Writing a long letter that repeats your CV

A Norwegian søknad is usually no longer than one page. A common mistake is to retell your whole career in full sentences. The CV already lists your jobs and education. The application should explain why this job and why you.

A simple structure that works well:

  1. Opening: which job you are applying for and why it interests you.
  2. Middle: two or three short paragraphs linking your experience to the most important requirements.
  3. Practical details: your Norwegian level, when you can start, if relevant.
  4. Closing: a short, polite sentence saying you would be glad to talk more.

If your letter goes onto a second page, cut it down. Remove general claims first and keep the concrete examples.

Mistake 3: Getting the tone wrong

Norwegian workplaces tend to value modesty, honesty and plain language. Two opposite mistakes are common.

Overselling:

I am the perfect candidate and will exceed all your expectations from day one.

Being overly formal or humble:

I would be most honoured if you would kindly consider my humble application.

Both can sound strange to a Norwegian reader. Aim for calm and factual instead:

I have worked as a care assistant for three years and I am used to night shifts and working closely with nurses. I would like to bring this experience to your team.

If the ad names a contact person, address the letter to them, for example Dear Ingrid Berg. Do not write Dear Sir/Madam when a name is given. Norwegian workplaces are fairly informal, and first names are widely used.

Mistake 4: Being vague or unrealistic about your Norwegian

Many ads ask for Norwegian language skills, even when the job itself can be done partly in English. Leaving your Norwegian out, or writing something vague like some Norwegian, makes the employer guess. Claiming a higher level than you have is worse, because it will show in the interview.

Be specific and honest. If you know your level on the CEFR scale (A1 to C2), use it. If not, use plain words and say what you are doing to improve.

Before:

I know a little Norwegian.

After:

My Norwegian is at a conversational level (around B1). I can talk with customers about everyday things, and I take an evening Norwegian course twice a week to improve.

If the job is clearly English-speaking, a short line is enough:

I speak fluent English and basic Norwegian, which I am actively improving.

Mistake 5: Missing the practical details in the ad

Good candidates are sometimes rejected for simple reasons. Read the whole ad carefully and check these points before you send anything:

  • Søknadsfrist: the application deadline. Apply in good time, because some employers start interviewing before the deadline.
  • How to apply: many ads ask you to apply through a form linked from the ad rather than by email. Follow the instructions given.
  • Documents requested: the ad may ask for vitnemål (diplomas) or attester (written references from previous employers). Attach them if asked.
  • Reference number: if the ad has one, include it.
  • Names and spelling: check the company name, the job title and the contact person's name.
  • File names: use clear names such as Anna_Kowalska_CV.pdf, not document_final2.pdf.

If a contact person is listed with a phone number, it can be a good idea to call with a real question about the job. Keep it short, and mention the call in your application if it gave you useful information.

Mistake 6: A CV that does not match Norwegian norms

Your application and CV are read together, so a weak CV can undo a good letter. Common fixes:

  • Use reverse chronological order, with your most recent job first.
  • Keep it to one to two pages.
  • A photo is optional. You do not need one.
  • Explain foreign education and job titles in simple terms, for example Bachelor's degree in Economics, Z University (Ukraine).
  • List references with name, role and phone number, or write that they are available on request.
  • Explain gaps briefly, for example 2023: moved to Norway, full-time Norwegian course.

Mistake 7: Applying for jobs without checking how well you fit

Sending dozens of applications to jobs you only half match can be tiring and discouraging. It often works better to send fewer, well-targeted applications. Before you write, compare the ad's requirements with your own experience. Ask yourself what you clearly have, what you partly have, and what is missing.

If you want a second opinion, you can paste the ad into ReadyCV and get an honest assessment of your strengths and gaps. Sometimes that means it is better not to apply. When you do apply and you are missing one requirement, say so honestly and show how you plan to close the gap:

I do not yet have a Norwegian driving licence, but I have booked my first lessons and expect to get it this spring.

Summary

Most job application mistakes in Norway are easy to fix once you know them. Write a short, specific søknad for each job, keep the tone honest and modest, and be clear about your Norwegian level. Follow the instructions in the ad and make sure your CV follows Norwegian norms. Focus on jobs where you can show a real match, and say openly what you are still working on.

Frequently asked questions

Can I write my job application in English if the ad is in Norwegian?

Often yes, especially if the job does not require fluent Norwegian. If the ad is in Norwegian and asks for good Norwegian skills, an English application can be a weak signal. If you are unsure, you can call the contact person and ask which language they prefer.

How long should a job application be in Norway?

Usually no more than one page. Employers expect a short, focused letter that links your experience to the job, not a full retelling of your CV.

Should I mention that my Norwegian is weak?

Yes, be honest about your level rather than hiding it. Describe it clearly, for example with the CEFR scale or words like 'basic' or 'conversational', and add what you are doing to improve. Employers generally respond better to honesty than to surprises in the interview.

What does 'søknadsfrist' mean in a job ad?

Søknadsfrist means the application deadline. Apply before this date, and preferably well in advance, as some employers begin looking at applications early.

Is it okay to call the contact person before applying?

In Norway it is common for ads to list a contact person, and a short call with a genuine question is usually seen as positive. Prepare one or two real questions and keep the call brief. Avoid calling just to say you are interested.
