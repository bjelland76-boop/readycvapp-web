Applying for a Job in Norway When You Don't Meet Every Requirement

Published November 23, 2026

You find a job ad on FINN.no or arbeidsplassen.no that looks right for you. Then you reach the list of qualifications and notice two or three points you cannot tick off. Should you apply anyway, or move on?

There is no single answer, but you can make a clear, honest decision instead of guessing. This article shows you how to sort the requirements in a Norwegian job ad, judge how close you are, recognise when it is wiser not to apply, and write about a gap without hiding it or making it bigger than it is.

Separate the must-haves from the nice-to-haves

Many Norwegian job ads (stillingsannonser) divide qualifications into requirements and wishes, though not always under clear headings. Look for these words:

  • Krav or kvalifikasjoner: requirements. These are what the employer needs.
  • Ønskelig, det er en fordel or vi ser gjerne at: desirable, an advantage, we would like. These are wishes, not conditions.
  • Personlige egenskaper: personal qualities, such as being reliable, flexible or good at working in a team.

If you only lack points from the "desirable" group, it is usually worth applying. Wishes describe the ideal candidate, and the ideal candidate rarely exists. If you lack points from the requirements group, read more carefully before you decide. For a deeper look at the wording, see how to read a Norwegian job ad.

A simple test: how close are you?

Write down every requirement in the ad. Next to each one, mark it in one of three ways:

  1. Yes: you have it and can show it on your CV.
  2. Partly: you have something similar, or you are close (for example, two years of experience where the ad asks for three).
  3. No: you do not have it.

Then ask yourself these questions:

  • Are most of your marks "yes" or "partly"?
  • Are your "no" points things you can learn on the job, such as a specific computer system or an internal routine?
  • Do you have a clear reason for wanting this exact job, beyond "I need a job"?

If you can answer yes to these, applying is usually reasonable. If most of your marks are "no", your time is probably better spent on an ad that fits you better.

Example: Amir is looking at a warehouse job at Y AS. The ad requires at least three years of warehouse experience, a forklift licence and "good Norwegian, spoken and written". Experience with a warehouse management system is listed as desirable. Amir has worked in a warehouse for two years in his home country and has a forklift licence. His Norwegian is conversational, but his writing is weaker. He has never used the system they mention. That is one clear yes, two partly, and one missing wish. Amir is close enough to apply.

Be honest about your Norwegian level

Many ads in Norway ask for Norwegian language skills, and this is often the requirement international applicants worry about most. Do not guess what the employer "really" means. Describe your level honestly and let the employer decide.

  • If you know your CEFR level (A1 to C2), use it: "Norwegian: B1 (spoken), A2 (written)".
  • If you do not know your level, use plain words: basic, conversational, fluent.
  • Say what you are doing to improve: a course, daily practice at work, a language café.
My Norwegian is conversational, and I am taking an evening course to improve my written Norwegian.

If the job involves a lot of contact with customers, patients or children, and you can hardly speak Norwegian yet, be realistic. In some jobs, you can learn the language as you go. Others depend on clear communication from the first day.

When it is better not to apply

Applying "just in case" to every ad is not a good strategy. It costs you time, and a stream of poorly matched applications can make you feel more rejected than you need to. Skip the ad when:

  • The job requires a specific licence, certificate or professional authorisation that you do not have, and the ad says it is necessary.
  • The ad asks for a degree or trade certificate in a field you have not studied, and your experience does not make up for it.
  • You lack most of the listed requirements, not just one or two.
  • You would have to stretch the truth in your CV or søknad to look like a match.

The last point matters a lot in Norway. Employers value honesty and often check references. Claiming skills you do not have is likely to come out in the interview or in your first weeks at work.

How to write about a gap in your søknad

The Norwegian job application (søknad) is usually no longer than one page, direct and modest. You do not need to list everything you lack, and missing wishes rarely need a mention. Focus on what you bring. If there is one important requirement you do not fully meet, mention it briefly and show why you are still close.

Here is how Amir could handle the experience requirement.

Before:

Although I do not have the three years of experience you require, I hope you will still consider me. I am a fast learner and very motivated.

After:

I have two years of experience from a large warehouse, where I received incoming goods, used a forklift daily and trained two new colleagues. That is one year less than you ask for, but I have worked with the same tasks your ad describes, and I am ready to take on full responsibility from the start.

The second version is just as honest about the missing year, but it leads with what Amir can do and gives evidence instead of asking for sympathy. Avoid phrases such as "I know I am not the perfect candidate". Let the employer judge that.

If the ad names a contact person, it is often wise to call or email before the deadline (søknadsfrist) with a short, specific question:

Hi Kari, I am interested in the warehouse job. I have two years of warehouse experience where the ad asks for three, a forklift licence, and conversational Norwegian. Would that be enough to be considered?

This gives you a real answer and shows initiative. Mention the conversation in your søknad if it was useful. For more on structure, see what to write in a Norwegian cover letter.

Let your CV close the distance

Your CV should make it easy for the employer to see how close you are. A few practical steps:

  • Use the same words as the ad where they truthfully describe your experience. If the ad says "customer service", do not only write "front desk".
  • Put the most relevant tasks near the top of each job entry.
  • Include courses, volunteer work or short certificates that cover part of a requirement.
  • Keep the standard Norwegian format: reverse chronological order, usually one to two pages, references with name, role and phone number or "available on request".

Summary

  • Sort the ad into requirements (krav) and wishes (ønskelig). Missing wishes is rarely a reason not to apply.
  • Mark each requirement as yes, partly or no. Apply if you are close and the gaps can be learned.
  • Describe your Norwegian level honestly, and say how you are improving.
  • Skip ads that require a licence or qualification you lack, or where you would need to stretch the truth.
  • In your søknad, lead with what you offer and mention an important missing requirement briefly, with evidence of why you are close.

Frequently asked questions

Is it rude to apply for a job in Norway if I don't meet all the requirements?

No. It is normal to apply when you match most, but not all, of the list. What employers do not appreciate is applicants who pretend to have skills they lack. Be honest about where you stand, and applying is perfectly acceptable.

The ad is in Norwegian, but my Norwegian is basic. Can I apply in English?

If the ad is written in Norwegian and asks for Norwegian skills, an English application may not be a good fit for that job. If the ad is in English or says English is the working language, apply in English. When in doubt, ask the contact person before the deadline.

Should I mention in my søknad that I am missing a requirement?

Only if it is an important requirement the employer will notice anyway, such as fewer years of experience than the ad asks for. In that case, mention it briefly and show why you are still close. Missing wishes (ønskelig) usually do not need a mention, and you should not spend the letter apologising for what you lack.

What does 'ønskelig' mean in a Norwegian job ad?

It means 'desirable'. Qualifications listed as ønskelig are wishes, not conditions. If you lack them but meet the main requirements, it is usually worth applying.

Is it a good idea to call the contact person before applying?

Often, yes, as long as you have a specific question, for example whether your experience or Norwegian level would be enough. Keep it short and polite. A useful conversation can also give you something concrete to mention in your søknad.
