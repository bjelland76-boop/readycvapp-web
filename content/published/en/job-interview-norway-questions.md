Job interviews in Norway often feel relaxed. You may be offered coffee, everyone uses first names, and the conversation can seem informal. Do not let that fool you. Behind the friendly tone, the employer is checking whether you can do the job, whether you will fit into the team and whether you are honest about yourself. This guide covers the questions international applicants are most likely to hear and gives you answer patterns to adapt.

## What a Norwegian job interview is usually like

Knowing the style helps you choose the right tone for your answers.

- **Flat structure.** The manager may introduce themselves by first name and ask you to do the same. Speak to everyone on the panel as equals.
- **Small panels.** You will often meet the hiring manager plus a future colleague or someone from HR. Sometimes an employee representative (*tillitsvalgt*) also takes part.
- **Modesty with substance.** Big claims such as “I am the best salesperson you will ever meet” tend to sound odd. Calm, concrete examples work much better. Do not undersell yourself either. Saying clearly what you have done is not bragging.
- **Cooperation matters.** Many employers ask how you work with others, share information and take initiative without waiting to be told.

Preparation starts with the job ad (*stillingsannonse*). Write down every requirement and every “we would like you to have” point. Next to each one, note a real example from your work, studies or voluntary work. ## “Tell us a bit about yourself” and “Why this job?”

These two questions often come first, and they set the tone for the rest of the interview.

### Tell us a bit about yourself

Keep it to one or two minutes. Leave out your life story. A simple pattern is **now – before – next**: what you do now, what you did before that is relevant, and why this job is the logical next step.

> I'm currently finishing a master's degree in logistics at Z University and working part-time at a warehouse in the evenings. Before I moved to Norway, I worked in order planning at a transport company for three years. Now I want to combine that experience with what I've learned here, which is why this planner role at Y AS caught my attention.

### Why do you want to work here?

A vague answer is a missed chance. Show that you have read the ad and know something about the workplace.

**Before:** “I like your company and I want to grow.”

**After:**

> The ad mentions that you are moving to a new warehouse system this year. I took part in a similar change in my last job, and I enjoy that kind of work: finding errors, training colleagues and making routines work in practice.

## “How good is your Norwegian?”

If you are applying in English, expect this question, even for jobs where English is the working language. Honesty is the only safe strategy, because the panel may switch to Norwegian to see how you cope.

Describe your level clearly. Use the CEFR scale (A1–C2) if you know your level, or plain words such as “basic”, “conversational” or “fluent”. Then say what you are doing to improve.

> My Norwegian is at about B1. I can handle everyday conversations and read most work emails, but I'm still slower in meetings. I take an evening course twice a week, and I'd be happy to use Norwegian with colleagues as much as possible.

If you can, say one or two sentences in Norwegian. It shows effort. Do not claim a level you cannot back up in the room.

## “Tell us about a time when…”

Many Norwegian employers ask competence-based questions. They want real examples, not theory. Typical versions:

- Tell us about a time you disagreed with a colleague.
- Tell us about a mistake you made at work.
- Tell us about a time you had too many tasks at once.
- Tell us about a time you took initiative.

Use four steps: **situation, task, action, result**. Keep the focus on what *you* did. End with what you learned.

> At a care home where I worked as an assistant, a resident kept refusing help with meals during the evening shift. My task was to make sure she ate, without forcing her. I spoke with her daughter and learned that she had always eaten later and alone. I suggested to my team leader that we change her mealtime. After that she ate without problems, and we added the information to her care plan so everyone on the shift knew.

This kind of answer shows initiative, cooperation and respect for routines. Those qualities fit well in most Norwegian workplaces.

## Your background, gaps and weaknesses

These questions can feel uncomfortable, but a short, honest answer usually works best.

**Experience from abroad.** The panel may not know your former employer or your education system. Explain what you actually did and at what level.

> In Poland I was a shift leader at a food factory, responsible for eight people and the production plan for each shift.

**Gaps in your CV.** Say what happened, briefly, and move on to what you did during that time.

> I had a gap of about a year when I moved to Norway. I used that time to learn Norwegian and did voluntary work at a second-hand shop run by a local organisation.

**Your weaknesses.** Pick a real one that will not stop you doing the job, and show how you manage it. Avoid disguised strengths such as “I work too hard”.

> I can be too quiet in meetings, especially in Norwegian. I've started preparing one or two points in advance so I make sure I contribute.

**Salary expectations.** If this comes up, have a range ready. Base it on similar job ads and on what people in the same field tell you. It is also fine to ask what range they have in mind for the role.

## Your questions to them, and what happens next

At the end, you will almost always hear “Do you have any questions for us?” Say yes. Good questions show real interest:

- What would a normal week in this role look like?
- How does the team share work and information?
- What will be most important in the first few months?
- What are the next steps in the process?

After the interview, the employer often contacts your references (*referanser*). Tell your references in advance which job you have applied for, so they are not surprised by the call. A short thank-you email the same day is a polite touch and gives you a chance to repeat why you want the job.

## Summary

- Expect a relaxed tone, but prepare as carefully as for a formal interview.
- Go through the job ad point by point and find a real example for each requirement.
- Keep “tell us about yourself” short: now, before, next.
- Be honest and specific about your Norwegian level, and show how you are improving.
- Answer “tell us about a time” questions with situation, task, action and result.
- Handle gaps and weaknesses briefly and honestly.
- Always have questions ready, and let your references know they may be contacted.

### Will the interview be in English or Norwegian?

It depends on the workplace and the role. If the ad is in English, the interview is often in English too, but the panel may switch to Norwegian for a while to hear your level. You can ask the contact person beforehand which language they plan to use.

### Should I bring a printed CV and documents?

It is wise to bring a printed copy of your CV and application, plus copies of diplomas or certificates that are relevant to the job. The panel may already have everything, but it helps if they want to check a detail. It also gives you something to look at if you lose your train of thought.

### Is it okay to say “I don't know” in an interview?

Yes, if you say it honestly and show how you would find the answer. In Norway, admitting what you don't know usually goes down better than guessing. For example: “I haven't used that system, but I learned a similar one quickly in my last job.”

### How should I dress for a job interview in Norway?

Dress neatly and a little smarter than people in the job would normally dress. For an office job, clean trousers and a shirt or blouse are usually enough. For a warehouse or construction job, smart casual is fine. A full suit is rarely expected unless the workplace is formal.

### What if the interview is on video?

Test your camera, microphone and the link well before the start. Sit somewhere quiet with light on your face, and keep your CV and the job ad open beside you. Treat it like a meeting in person: be on time and dress as you would for the office.
