A strong candidate can still send a weak application. The cover letter could have gone to any company, the CV lists tasks without showing what they led to, or a habit from back home confuses an employer in another country. Each of these is fixable, often in a single evening, once you know what to look for.

This guide covers six job application mistakes and how to correct them. It is written for anyone applying in English, whether you are applying locally, moving abroad or working in a language that is not your first.

## Sending the same application to every employer

One CV and one letter for every job saves time, but it often shows. A generic letter talks about "a dynamic company" and "an exciting opportunity" without naming anything that belongs to this employer.

**Before:**

> I am writing to apply for the position at your company. I am a hard-working team player with excellent communication skills and I believe I would be a great fit.

**After:**

> I am applying for the Customer Support Lead role because your team supports customers in Spanish, Portuguese and English. For the past two years I have done exactly that at a travel booking company in Lisbon, where I also trained new starters.

The fix: keep a master CV with everything you have done. For each job, cut and reorder it so the most relevant experience comes first. Then write a fresh opening paragraph for every cover letter that names the role and gives one concrete reason you fit it.

## Not answering what the ad actually asks for

A job ad is a list of the employer's needs. If a warehouse supervisor ad asks for stock control, supplier contact and a forklift licence, your application should show all three clearly. A common trap is writing about what you are proud of rather than what the employer asked for.

A quick way to check:

1. Copy the ad and highlight every requirement and every "desirable" skill.
2. Find where your CV or letter gives evidence for each highlighted point.
3. For any point without evidence, add a real example or accept that it is a gap.
4. If you cover most of the essentials, mention the gap honestly in your letter.

An honest gap sentence might look like this:

> I have not yet used SAP, but I learned two other inventory systems quickly in my current role and I am comfortable picking up new software.

Sometimes this exercise shows that a job is not a good match. That is useful to know, because your time is better spent on roles where you can show real evidence. If you want a second opinion, you can paste the ad into [ReadyCV](https://app.aerlig.no/?utm_source=readycvapp&utm_medium=artikkel&utm_campaign=job-application-mistakes) and get an honest assessment of your strengths and what is missing.

## Listing duties instead of results

A CV that only lists responsibilities tells the reader what the job involved, not how well you did it. Two people with the same title can have very different track records, so show what changed because you were there.

**Before:**

- Responsible for social media accounts
- Handled customer emails
- Helped with events

**After:**

- Planned and posted weekly content for three social media accounts and started a monthly customer Q&A video
- Answered customer emails within one working day and wrote reply templates the team still uses
- Coordinated logistics for two product launch events

You do not need impressive figures. Use action verbs, say what you improved or created, and add concrete details: how often, for whom, with what outcome.

## Assuming your home country's conventions travel

The rules you learned at home may not apply elsewhere. Conventions differ between countries, and sometimes between industries in the same country. The document may be called a CV in one place and a résumé in another. Some employers expect a photo and date of birth; others would rather you left personal details out. Expected length ranges from a strict one page to two pages or more.

Research before you write:

- Read several job ads in that country and note what they ask you to include.
- Look at profiles of people in similar roles on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com) to see how they present their experience.
- Check the large job portals in that country, which often publish guidance for applicants.
- Apply in the language the ad is written in, unless it says otherwise.

When you are unsure, a clean one-to-two-page CV with no photo and only essential personal details is a cautious choice.

## Leaving the reader to decode your background

An employer abroad may not know your university, your former employer or what your job title means. A title translated word for word can sound odd, and local abbreviations mean nothing outside your country.

**Before:**

> 2019–2023: Specialist II, OOO Severtrans

**After:**

> 2019–2023: Logistics Planner, Severtrans (regional freight company, around 200 staff), Kazan, Russia

A few habits help:

- Add a short description after unfamiliar company names: what they do and roughly how big they are.
- Use the job title that best describes your real work, not a literal translation.
- Name your qualification and add a plain-English equivalent, for example *Licenciatura in Nursing (four-year degree)*.
- If your right to work is relevant, state your status briefly and accurately, such as *Holds a valid work permit for this country* or *Will require sponsorship*.
- Write your phone number with the country code and mention your time zone if you are applying from abroad.

## Careless details and missed instructions

A wrong company name or a skipped instruction can undo a good application. Writing "I would love to join Nordway Logistics" in a letter to a different company suggests you are sending applications in bulk. Sending a CV by email when the ad asks for an online form can mean it never reaches the right person.

Read the ad twice: once for the role, and once only for the instructions. Then run through this checklist:

- Company name, job title and contact person are correct everywhere.
- You have included everything the ad asks for, such as a cover letter or reference number.
- Your email address looks professional, ideally based on your name.
- Your files have clear names, such as *Priya-Nair-CV.pdf*, not *final_v3_NEW.docx*.
- Dates use one consistent format throughout.
- You have read the text aloud once, slowly, to catch missing words.

If English is not your first language, ask someone you trust to proofread, or leave the text overnight and read it again with fresh eyes. If an instruction is genuinely unclear, a short, polite question to the contact person is usually fine.

## Summary

Many of these mistakes are easy to catch once you slow down before pressing send. Tailor your CV and letter to each role, match your evidence to the requirements, describe results rather than duties, and check the conventions of the country you are applying to. Make your background easy to understand for a reader who does not know your country, then do a final check of names, details and instructions.

### Should I apply if I do not meet every requirement?

Often, yes, as long as you meet most of the essential requirements. Ads frequently describe an ideal candidate, and employers may still consider people who come close. Be honest about the gap and show how you would close it.

### Is it a mistake to use the same CV for jobs in different countries?

It can be, because expectations around length, photos and personal details differ. Read local job ads and look at similar profiles to see what is normal there. Adjust your CV for each market rather than assuming one version works everywhere.

### Should I translate my job titles and qualifications?

Use the title that best describes what you actually did, rather than a word-for-word translation. For degrees, it is usually wise to keep the original name and add a short plain-English explanation. That way the employer understands the level and you stay accurate.

### Should I send my CV as a PDF or a Word file?

Follow the instructions in the ad first. If none are given, a PDF is usually a safe choice because the layout stays the same on any device. Some application systems prefer Word files, so check the upload form if there is one.

### How do I know if my cover letter is too generic?

Swap the company name for a competitor's. If the letter still makes sense, it is too generic. Add details about the role, the team or the employer's work that only fit this application.
