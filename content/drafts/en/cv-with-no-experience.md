Staring at a blank page because you have never had a proper job is a familiar feeling. Whether you are leaving school, finishing university or moving to a new country, you probably have more to offer than you think. A CV with no experience is not an empty CV. It is a CV that shows **potential**: what you have learnt, what you have done and how you behave when someone gives you responsibility.

This guide shows you how to build that kind of CV step by step, with examples you can adapt.

## Choose a layout that puts your strengths first

A traditional CV lists work history at the top. If you have little or none, that layout works against you. Change the order so the reader meets your strongest material first.

A layout that often works well for first-time job seekers:

1. Name and contact details
2. Short profile (three or four lines)
3. Education
4. Projects, volunteering and other experience
5. Skills
6. Languages, interests and anything else relevant

Keep it to one page. With limited experience, a tight single page looks confident, while two thin pages can look padded. Use clear headings, a readable font and plenty of white space.

## Write a short profile that looks forward

The profile at the top is your chance to explain who you are and what you want. Without work history, focus on direction, motivation and one or two concrete strengths.

Before:

> Hard-working and motivated person looking for any job.

After:

> Recent business graduate with strong spreadsheet skills and experience organising large student events. Looking for an entry-level role in operations or customer support where I can learn quickly and take on responsibility.

The second version is specific. It names a field, shows evidence and tells the employer what kind of role you want. Avoid empty words such as "dynamic" or "passionate" unless you immediately back them up.

## Make your education do more of the work

When education is your main qualification, give it more detail than an experienced candidate would.

Instead of just the name of the course and the dates, add:

- Relevant modules or subjects
- A final project, dissertation or major assignment
- Group work where you had a clear role
- Awards, scholarships or good results, if they are genuinely strong

Example:

> **BSc Computer Science**, 2021–2024
> Final project: built a booking app for a local sports club, used by members to reserve courts. Led a small team on a database design assignment.

If you are still at school, the same principle applies. Mention subjects connected to the job and anything you did beyond lessons, such as a school newspaper, student council or a science competition.

## Count everything that is experience in disguise

Many people leave out valuable experience because it was unpaid or informal. Employers hiring for entry-level roles are often more interested in what you did and how you did it than in whether you were paid.

Things that can belong on your CV:

- **Volunteering**: food banks, charity shops, festivals, refugee support, animal shelters
- **Part-time and casual work**: babysitting, tutoring, deliveries, a weekend café shift, helping in a family business
- **Personal projects**: a blog, a small online shop, a video channel, an app or a website
- **Clubs and sports**: team captain, coach for younger players, club treasurer
- **Responsibilities at home**: caring for a relative or managing a household can show reliability and organisation, if you are comfortable mentioning it

Describe each one like a job, with what you did and what came out of it.

Before:

> Volunteer at a charity shop.

After:

> **Volunteer, local charity shop**, Saturdays, 2023–2024
> Served customers at the till, sorted and priced donations, and trained new volunteers on shop routines.

The second version shows customer service, handling money and the trust to train others.

## Show your skills with evidence

A list of skills on its own proves little. Anyone can write "good communicator". Link each skill to something you have actually done.

Weak:

> Teamwork, communication, problem-solving

Stronger:

> **Teamwork**: coordinated a student group on a market research project and presented the results to the class.
> **Communication**: tutored secondary-school pupils in maths every week for a year.
> **Digital skills**: Excel (pivot tables, formulas), Canva, basic HTML.

Languages are a real asset, especially if you are applying abroad. List each language with an honest level, such as native, fluent, professional working proficiency or basic. Never overstate a level you cannot back up in an interview.

## Tailor it to each job and check local conventions

Sending the same CV to every employer is tempting, but a tailored version is usually more convincing. Read the job ad carefully, note the skills and qualities they ask for, and make sure your CV shows evidence of those, ideally using similar wording.

For example, if Amara is applying for a hotel receptionist role, she might move her customer-facing volunteering to the top and mention her French and Spanish. For a data-entry role, she would put her spreadsheet coursework first instead.

If you are applying in another country, remember that CV conventions differ. Some places say "CV", others say "résumé" and expect a slightly different format. In some countries a photo is common, while in others it is better left out. Expected length and whether to include your date of birth also vary. Look for advice from local career services, study example CVs from that country and read the job ad closely. Each country has its own large job portals, and [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com) is used in many countries, so it is worth keeping your profile there consistent with your CV.

## Final checks before you send it

Run through this short checklist:

- Is your email address professional (ideally your name, not a nickname)?
- Does every section show something useful, not filler?
- Have you used action verbs such as *organised*, *built*, *served*, *trained*, *led*?
- Are dates and formatting consistent throughout?
- Has someone else proofread it for spelling and grammar?
- Is the file saved as a PDF with a clear name, such as *Daniel-Okafor-CV.pdf*?

## Summary

Writing a CV with no experience is about showing what you can do, not apologising for what you have not done yet. Put your education and strongest activities first, write a focused profile, describe volunteering, projects and casual work as real experience, and back up every skill with an example. Tailor each version to the job and to the country you are applying in. A clear, honest, one-page CV built this way gives an employer good reasons to take a chance on you.

### Should I leave a section empty if I have no work experience?

No, simply leave out the work experience heading and use something like "Experience" or "Projects and volunteering" instead. This lets you include unpaid and informal activities without drawing attention to a gap. The goal is for every heading on the page to contain something useful.

### How long should a CV with no experience be?

One page is usually enough and often looks stronger than two. Focus on quality rather than quantity, and cut anything that does not support the job you are applying for. Conventions vary by country, so check local expectations if you are applying abroad.

### Can I include school results or grades?

Yes, if they are good and relevant, especially when you are early in your career. Strong results in subjects linked to the job can help show ability. If your grades are average, it is often better to highlight projects, coursework or activities instead.

### Do I need a cover letter if I have no experience?

A cover letter is especially helpful when your CV is short, because it lets you explain your motivation and why you suit the role. Many employers ask for one, and even when it is optional it can set you apart. Keep it specific to the job rather than using a generic template.

### Is it fine to mention hobbies on my CV?

Hobbies can help if they show relevant skills or say something meaningful about you, such as running a club, competing in a sport or building things in your free time. Generic interests like "reading" or "travelling" add little unless you give detail. Keep this section short and near the end.
